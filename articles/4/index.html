FSK in a nutshell: tones and phase considerations

In this article, we'll take a quick look at FSK, one of the most common digital modulation techniques. We'll start with the core idea, dive into the math and bridge the gap between textbook theory and real-world implementation.

1. FSK definition

Let's start with the acronym: FSK stands for Frequency-Shift Keying.
It's a digital modulation technique where information is encoded by shifting the frequency of a signal in discrete steps.

Let's kick things off with the simplest case: Binary FSK (or BFSK or 2FSK), where the carrier is shifted between two discrete frequencies to transmit binary data (\(0\)s and \(1\)s).
Say we choose two frequencies, \(f_0\) and \(f_1\) and we want to transmit the following bit string: \(0101\).
In the real domain, the first and the second signals will be described by: \[x_0(t) = \cos(2\pi f_0 t), x_1(t) = \cos(2\pi f_1 t)\] Assuming we choose signal \(x_0\) to transmit a zero and \(x_1\) to transmit a one, we'll get a signal looking like:

Notice, however, the jumps at some bit boundaries in the waveform above. This is far from ideal: an abrupt change in the signal is, mathematically, a very sudden event, and sudden events need a lot of frequency content to be described. The result is spectral splatter (often loosely called spectral leakage): instead of the clean spectrum we saw earlier, we get something like this:

Why is this a problem?

There are two main reasons.

  • Wasted bandwidth: The signal occupies much more spectrum than the two tones alone would require. If we transmit it, the energy spilling outside the intended band interferes with neighbouring channels and regulations typically limit how much power a transmitter may emit out of band.
  • Harder detection: The energy that splatters around \(f_0\) and \(f_1\) ends up in the very frequencies we are monitoring, which reduces the margin between "tone present" and "tone absent".
Can we get rid of the jumps? Yes, by making sure the phase of the signal never jumps when the frequency changes. That is the subject of the next section.
